# I

Our world is changing, and it's changing rapidly. I'm sure you already know about the whole advent of AI and how it's transforming every area of life, but I don't think you know just how radical of a change it's gonna bring, especially in 2027. Just recently, the Riemann Hypothesis was solved by Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge, or rather the unreleased Claude model that he was working with. In fact, he claims to have proven the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis as well, and the proof was formalized in the Lean language and independently verified to be true (17 **million** lines of code, btw).

I'm telling you this because, just two or three years ago, AI models struggled with elementary and middle school math; they would regularly fail at simple problems like "2+7", and now the acceleration from that level to essentially a superhuman mathematician has been a bit hard to grasp. Now, we're on the cusp of recursive self-improvement, or RSI.

> Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI):
>
> The inflection point in time at which AI models can make better, smarter versions of themselves by automating AI research, thus kicking off the Intelligence Explosion.

A lot of people are saying that major labs like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind have achieved RSI, but we can't know for sure unless that claim is independently verified. The good thing, though, is that once we *do* get RSI, AI progress is gonna accelerate even more than it already has, and we'll get AGI (Artificial General Intelligence; something as smart as the best humans in any cognitive field) and soon after, ASI (Artificial Superintelligence, smarter than the entire human population combined). Right now, at the pace that we're moving at, I expect AGI in early to mid-2027, and perhaps ASI in 2028. But until we can independently demonstrate and quantify this progress, we won't know for sure.

Now, I'm in high school right now. I just finished up my college applications, and my original plan was to go into AI research and spearhead the effort to AGI/ASI. But seeing that both of those milestones will likely be reached before I even graduate college, I've changed my plans. As the field is growing, I'm wondering one thing: must these extraordinarily powerful systems require equally extraordinary computational resources?

# II

Right now, the entire AI field is asking one question: how do we get to AGI? How intelligent of a system can we make with the resources available? That's good; I'm not suggesting we should slow down AI progress at all.

But perhaps once we get AGI/ASI, a better question to ask would be **how little computation do we actually need to achieve a given level of intelligence?**

This brings me to a term I'd like to introduce: **Compact Intelligence (CI).**

This would be the pursuit of achieving highly generalizable intelligence under extreme computational constraints, with a *massive emphasis* on preserving reasoning, adaptability, and the ability to learn.

CI isn't simply quantizing a large model, pruning unnecessary parameters, or distilling a large model's reasoning into a smaller package, though all three of those techniques certainly work well. My proposed research objective is broader, and it would build upon existing research in efficient machine learning.

To give you an idea of what I mean, here's a fictional scenario.

Imagine it's the year 2029. You just downloaded Sable-3, a small language model released by Sable Labs, on your computer to run locally. This model's small, like *really* small—not even the equivalent of a 10B-parameter model from 2026. I say equivalent because this model isn't built on the standard transformer architecture that earlier generations of AI models used. This is something completely novel, something that has expert-level reasoning capability in STEM, specifically:

- Science: it can model theoretical experiments and phenomena, and reason from first principles
- Technology: it can create new algorithms, code production-grade software, and solve complex computational problems
- Engineering: it can perform technical design, optimize machines, and simulate them in virtual environments
- Mathematics: it can write advanced proofs and solve unfamiliar problems

Essentially, you have AGI's STEM ability in something small enough to run on your MacBook. But there's an important distinction I'd like to make: a small model isn't necessarily efficient if it takes much longer than a larger model to produce the same results. CI should account for not just model size but also active computation, memory requirements, latency, and reliability.

# III

Here's something that I realized.

**A sufficiently small model might rely more heavily on compact, reusable abstractions because it cannot afford to represent every pattern independently.**

Now you might be asking, *what the hell does that even mean?* Don't worry, I gotchu.

A large model has enough capacity to store enormous amounts of specialized patterns. A small model has nowhere near that amount of storage space.

To achieve comparable performance, therefore, it would have to identify the deeper, general structures shared across many problems. The very size constraint might encourage the model to generalize and develop more efficient abstractions across its training data.

The only problem here is that we don't know for sure that small models develop superior abstractions. Capacity constraints could also cause information loss, brittle shortcuts, and weaker reasoning; verification is needed.

However, if we *do* produce a small model that we know has developed a genuinely outstanding understanding of STEM fields, then it could be used as a teacher for students.

Take this, for example: let's say we have a student named Will, who's a high school junior learning calculus right now. Will might approach calculus by memorizing the various differentiation/integration rules, practicing integration techniques, and solving application problems. Alternatively, Will could organize much of his *understanding* around the foundational ideas, namely local linearity, accumulation, and the relationship between differentiation and integration. The fundamental concepts would allow him to quickly reconstruct and connect many individual concepts, after which he could go back to his original method of memorizing and practicing.

Couldn't a small model develop similarly economical representations of what it learns? Could those representations transfer to intellectually demanding fields like physics, computer science, and engineering? And could we identify the mechanisms responsible?

Let's go back to Sable-3, and suppose that it was trained to solve mathematical problems using highly reusable structures. If Sable could make its strategies understandable to humans, they might be valuable teaching strategies, particularly for Will.

He could use Sable to build a small set of first-principles abstractions and derive unfamiliar solutions independently of the AI's help, rather than just memorizing procedures. This wouldn't just make him a better student. It could make him a better mathematical thinker, capable of applying calculus to genuinely difficult real-world problems.

This whole process could be quantified by **learning efficiency:** how much transferable understanding a student acquires per unit of instructional effort. But it's important to distinguish between three things:

1. Computationally efficient model representations
2. Human-understandable explanations
3. Teaching strategies that are proven to produce durable learning and transfer

These are related possibilities, not equivalent properties. A model might reason really efficiently but fail to teach its student. Therefore, this hypothesis would require experimental testing.

Here's something cool, though: maybe the ultimate objective of CI is not to compress everything an intelligent system already knows. Maybe it's to compress the mechanisms that allow a system to acquire new knowledge, reason with it, and apply it to unfamiliar problems.

Let's take two models, for example: Model A and Model B, both built by Sable Labs.

The researchers developed Model A to be essentially a compressed representation of the best large-size frontier models, and it possesses expert-level knowledge on virtually every STEM subject. 22.4B parameters.

Let's contrast that with Model B, which is smaller than Model A and was developed to rapidly learn unfamiliar topics, develop new abstractions, structures, and relationships on the fly, and verify its results. 8.4B parameters.

Which one would you rather use? I think the answer is obvious. If something like Model B is possible, then that's a more ambitious direction of general intelligence that I, personally at least, would want to pursue.

# IV

I don't know how CI might be achieved. I suspect we might have to move beyond traditional language model architectures and develop something more robust, capable of reasoning in ways we can better understand and verify. Existing techniques like distillation and reinforcement learning may transfer sophisticated procedures over to smaller models, and access to external tools could also allow compact models to solve difficult problems reliably. There are still some questions that, if solved, could greatly speed up the process of development.

> How much intelligence is fundamentally compressible?
>
> Can reasoning strategies transfer across domains?
>
> Are there irreducible computational requirements for general intelligence?
>
> Can the ability to learn itself be distilled?

These questions make CI a research agenda rather than just a prediction of the future.

I also think that the advent of AGI/ASI would make CI more important, not less. If increasingly capable AI systems automate AI research, it would initially seem like AI researchers are out of a job. But greater intelligence could also open up new avenues for researching efficient intelligence, and humans could still have meaningful roles in that pursuit.

This process would likely create a feedback loop that goes something like this:

**More Capable AI → Better Compression Research → More Efficient Intelligence → Broader Access to Powerful AI & More Research**

For decades, AI research has explored what becomes possible as computational resources increase. CI asks a complementary question: what can be achieved when we aggressively reduce the resources required for intelligence?

The significance of this extends beyond running smart models on your computer. It concerns the accessibility of intelligence, the fundamental structure of reasoning (note to self: maybe Immanuel Kant has the answer?), and maybe even our complete understanding of how humans truly learn.

*What if the next great breakthrough in artificial intelligence isn't discovering how to make intelligence bigger, but discovering just how small it can become?*
